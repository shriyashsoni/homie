PART I — THE IDEA
1. The core idea
Find My Homie is a compatibility-first shared-living platform. It helps people find a room, find people to rent with, or fill a spare room by combining practical housing constraints with the things that make day-to-day living work: routines, cleanliness, guests, food, boundaries, money, culture and communication.
The core promise remains: “Find your person, then your place.” FMH should not stop at a match score. It should help people progress from intent to a relevant shortlist, a mutually accepted connection, a viewing, clear terms and—if all goes well—a move-in.
1.1 The problem
Property portals help people find rooms but usually do little to explain whether the people sharing the home are compatible.
Social groups can have useful supply but are noisy, difficult to search, vulnerable to stale posts and dependent on repeated direct messages.
Renters may not know which questions to ask before viewing or paying a deposit.
Hosts and current tenants repeat the same details to every enquiry, juggle viewing times and struggle to compare applicants fairly.
The lived experience of sharing a home depends on ordinary details that listings often omit: guests, cooking, cleaning, quiet hours, prayer, privacy, shared expenses and boundaries.
In India, food practices, vegetarian/non-vegetarian cooking, religion-related routines, family involvement and different household expectations can materially affect whether a home is a fit.
Language is not a reliable proxy for intent: people mix Kannada, Hindi, English and other languages, use slang and voice notes, and change their minds mid-conversation.

1.3 Why launch in Bengaluru
Bengaluru is the initial market hypothesis because it has a substantial population of working professionals, people relocating for jobs, shared accommodation demand and a culture of using digital services. This is a strategic hypothesis, not proof of product-market fit. Launch in a focused cluster or two, recruit supply first, and expand locality coverage only when there are enough current listings and seekers to produce useful results.
1.4 Product principles
People first, property second—but do not force everyone into a buddy-up journey. Someone who needs a room urgently should be able to search rooms immediately.
One user, one canonical profile, multiple interfaces.
Ask only what is needed at that point in the journey.
Separate practical feasibility from lifestyle compatibility.
Treat food and household practices as real compatibility factors, not afterthoughts.
Explain recommendations in plain language and show meaningful differences.
Make uncertainty visible. Never invent certainty from incomplete answers.
Privacy by default; users choose what is published and shared.
Hosts need value too: fewer irrelevant enquiries, easier scheduling, clearer costs and faster vacancy filling.
Measure housing outcomes, not just quiz completion or time spent chatting.
Start with rules and human-reviewable processes; add complexity only when data justifies it.

2.3 Important branch: urgent room search
Do not make a user finish every personality question before seeing rooms. Capture city/area, budget, move-in date and room type first; allow an initial shortlist with a clearly labelled low-confidence fit. Encourage completion of lifestyle and household preferences before contacting a host. This preserves usefulness for urgent renters without pretending that a partial profile is complete.

3.1 What “understanding natural language” means
Homie must interpret the user's meaning, not simply match keywords. “I’m vegetarian but don’t care if flatmates eat meat outside” is different from “I’m okay with meat being cooked in the shared kitchen.” “Guests are fine, just no random people sleeping over” is different from “I want a social house.” The system must preserve negation, exceptions, conditions and who a preference applies to.

3.3 Extraction rules
Use a strict schema and validate all model outputs before any write.
Do not allow a language model to execute arbitrary database writes. It proposes structured updates; backend rules authorise them.
High-confidence, low-impact preferences may be summarised for confirmation in a batch. Budget ceiling, move-in date, public visibility, social sharing and money terms require clear confirmation.
If a user corrects a fact, update the draft and show the changed field. Keep reversible history.
If the referent is unclear (“make it 20”), ask whether the user means budget, date or something else.
If a value is missing, keep it unknown; do not fill it with a plausible guess.
Don't infer religion, caste, dietary practice, gender, relationship status, income or safety from name, photo, accent, language or social account.
Let users edit every extracted field directly in the website.
Store enough provenance to debug mistakes; minimise retention of raw chat and audio.

3.5 WhatsApp-specific caution
WhatsApp Business Solution Terms published in 2026 restrict AI providers from using the Business Solution when AI is the primary functionality, subject to specific exceptions; WhatsApp also has a separate third-party-agent experience whose availability varies by country and whose chats with agents are not end-to-end encrypted in the same way as personal chats. This makes WhatsApp a channel to validate, not an assumption the architecture can depend on. Review the exact terms and available products with counsel/Meta before launch.
Build the website chat and shared backend first. Keep the messaging adapter replaceable. If WhatsApp is approved for the intended use, explain the data handling and encryption limitations before a user connects it. Do not use unofficial automation that risks account suspension.
3.6 Example conversational scripts
A. Seeker starts in casual English
User: “hey need a room around indiranagar, 15-18k max, moving next month. wfh 3 days. no party house pls but friends can come over lol”
Homie: “Got you. I’ve picked up Indiranagar, ₹15–18k and WFH three days a week. When you say ₹18k max, should I include maintenance and utilities in that limit? And what date are you hoping to move?”
User: “yeah total ideally. anytime after 1 nov”
Homie: “Done — total monthly budget around ₹18k, move-in from 1 November. I’ll look for quieter households where occasional guests are okay. Quick one: are you looking for a private room or would a shared room work too?”
B. Food boundaries
User: “im veg. dont want meat cooked at home but idc if others eat it outside”
Homie: “Understood: vegetarian preference, and your key household requirement is no meat cooked in the shared home. Is that a non-negotiable for you, or would separate cooking arrangements work?”
Why this works: the assistant captures the practical rule and asks whether it is strict. It does not assume the user's religion or judge anyone else's identity.
C. Voice note in a mixed language
User sends a Kannada-English voice note about budget, area and move-in date.
Homie: “I heard: near Whitefield, around ₹20k total, moving in mid-November. Did I get that right? You can edit any of these.”
The production version should respond in the user's selected language where quality is adequate. For a voice note, the UI should show a transcript and a correction action; if the transcript confidence is low, ask rather than persist uncertain fields.
D. Host creates a listing
Homie: “Let's get the room listed first. Which area, monthly rent, deposit, maintenance, move-in date and room type?”
After listing details: “What is your household like? I’ll ask a few quick questions so applicants know what living there is like.”
Host answers short questions about guests, cooking rules, quiet hours, cleaning and viewing availability. Homie then previews the listing and asks for explicit confirmation before publishing.
E. Correcting an earlier answer
User: “actually not indiranagar, closer to domlur. and 18k is just rent, maintenance can be extra”
Homie: “Updated the area to Domlur/nearby. I’ve changed your rent ceiling to ₹18k before maintenance, so the total monthly cost may be higher. Want me to cap total monthly spend separately?”

PART IV — THE QUIZ AND HOUSEHOLD PROFILE
4. Quiz philosophy
The quiz should feel like a conversation about living together, not a personality test from a magazine. Ask one question at a time, use everyday language, explain why a question matters where helpful, allow “it depends” or “not sure”, and ask adaptive follow-ups only where they meaningfully improve the match.
Target 15 core lifestyle questions plus no more than 5 adaptive follow-ups; housing essentials are separate fields. A user who urgently needs a room can see initial results after essential constraints and complete more questions before contacting a host. Hosts answer a short 5–7-question household-fit set because their listing already contains substantial information.

PART V — MATCHING SCIENCE AND BACKEND SCORING
5. The right mental model
Matching is not one number. The system must first determine whether a room/household is practically possible, then evaluate lifestyle fit, then explain trade-offs. The first version should be transparent and deterministic. We do not have enough outcome data to claim a validated psychometric instrument or train a reliable personalised ML ranker.
5.1 Three stages
Stage
Logic
Output
Stage 1: feasibility
City/locality overlap, budget and total costs, move-in window, room type, duration, occupancy/household constraints, listing availability, blocked/suspended state.
Eligible / not eligible / needs clarification.
Stage 2: compatibility
Compare lifestyle answers and household rules; apply versioned dimension weights; account for answer coverage and confidence.
Provisional compatibility score + coverage/confidence.
Stage 3: ranking and explanation
Apply freshness, availability, user priorities, response likelihood and diversity of results without hiding hard mismatches.
Ranked results with 2–3 grounded reasons and any important trade-off.

5.2 Hard constraints versus preferences
A hard constraint is a user-confirmed non-negotiable. A preference is something that can vary. Never turn every mismatch into a hard exclusion. Conversely, don't average away an explicit constraint such as total budget ceiling or a required household cooking rule.

5.4 Dimension scoring
For an ordinal response on a meaningful 1–5 scale, a simple initial similarity score can be:
sᵢ = 100 × (1 − |xᵢA − xᵢB| / 4)
This is only appropriate when equal distance between scale points is defensible. Many answers are categorical or multi-select, so they need dimension-specific compatibility tables—not a generic numeric conversion.
Example dimension
Illustrative scoring logic
Sleep
Similar sleep/quiet expectations score high; early vs late may be workable if noise expectations align. Do not score only by labels.
Food rules
Both allow the same practices = high. One requires no meat cooked at home and the other cooks meat daily = hard conflict if confirmed non-negotiable. Separate utensils may resolve a flexible mismatch.
Guests
Compare guest frequency, notice and overnight rules independently. A person who likes guests can still be compatible with someone who needs advance notice.
Chores
Rota vs informal style is not automatically incompatible; ask whether both can agree a clear system.
Expenses
Different tracking preferences can be compatible if fixed bills, payment dates and settlement process are agreed.
Religion/culture
Never score identity. Compare only user-stated household practices/space requirements that are relevant and voluntarily shared.

5.5 Weighted score with missing data
For comparable dimensions with weights wᵢ and similarity scores sᵢ:
S = Σ(wᵢ × sᵢ) / Σ(wᵢ for dimensions with comparable data)
Show data coverage separately:
Coverage = Σ(weights for comparable answered dimensions) / Σ(all configured weights)
A profile with a score of 84 and 40% coverage is not equivalent to a score of 84 with 95% coverage. Label it as a provisional fit and avoid a false sense of precision. If coverage is below a configured minimum, show “not enough information yet” or a qualitative fit instead of a percentage.
5.6 Confidence and uncertainty
Keep score, coverage and confidence as separate fields.
Confidence reflects data quality and certainty of answers, not the person's character.
Unknown answers are not automatically scored as mismatches.
For high-impact constraints, require explicit confirmation instead of trusting low-confidence extraction.
If the user gives contradictory answers, resolve them before using the field.
Avoid displaying decimal precision. A rounded score or qualitative band is more honest.
Use explanations grounded in the actual fields: “Both prefer quiet weekdays; your guest preferences differ, so discuss notice.”
5.7 Calibration and validation plan
Start with expert-designed rules and interview renters/hosts about what really causes household friction.
Run cognitive interviews: ask users to explain what each question means to them; rewrite ambiguous wording.
Pilot with a small cohort and compare predicted fit with user-rated relevance, accepted connections, attended viewings and move-in outcomes.
Collect outcome feedback separately from identity data; do not use a user's religion, caste, name, photo or language as a proxy feature.
Review false positives (high score but bad fit) and false negatives (low score but successful household) with qualitative notes.
Tune weights only when there is enough data; version every change and run a holdout comparison where feasible.
Test score stability: small changes in an answer should not cause dramatic ranking swings unless the answer crosses a genuine hard constraint.
Audit whether recommendations systematically disadvantage groups; consult legal and domain experts before adding any sensitive fields or filters.
5.8 Why not use ML immediately?
A machine-learning ranker needs representative data and meaningful outcomes. Early FMH will have sparse, biased and self-selected data; a model could learn harmful proxies or overfit to early users. Use transparent rules first. Consider learned ranking only after sufficient quality-labeled outcomes, clear evaluation criteria, bias testing and a rollback plan.
5.9 Example match explanation
“Potential fit: both prefer a calm home during the week, cook regularly and want clear chore expectations. One difference to discuss: one person is comfortable with guests several times a week, while the other prefers advance notice. Food/kitchen rules need confirmation before this match is marked as suitable.”
The system must not say “90% compatible means you'll get along” or imply scientific validation that has not happened.

6.2 Request design
Connection requests should not be generic “Hi” spam. Provide optional prompts based on the actual listing and profile, such as “I can move in during your availability window,” “I saw your household cooking rules and they work for me,” or “Could I clarify whether maintenance is included?” Avoid exposing sensitive quiz responses. Let users choose the fields they share.
6.3 Buddy-up groups
A user starts a group and selects target areas, total budget, move-in window and desired household size.
Invitees accept explicitly and can see the group's shared constraints; personal quiz answers remain private.
Group requirements are aggregated with permission. Show where members' budgets/areas overlap and where they conflict.
Group searches listings using combined constraints; don't simply average budgets if one member has a firm lower ceiling.
Members can leave or dissolve the group; access to shared data changes accordingly.
No member can expose another member's private details without consent.
6.4 In-product messaging
A basic connection inbox may be necessary for the web flow, but full real-time chat can be deferred. V1 can use structured connection requests, short messages and email/in-app notifications. If direct messaging is included, add report/block, rate limits, moderation signals, consent and contact-sharing controls from the beginning.
6.5 Parent/trusted supporter workflow
The adult seeker owns the account and decision.
The seeker can share a selected listing summary or a revocable Homie Card with a parent/trusted supporter.
Shared views can include disclosed rent, deposit, brokerage, locality, viewing time, host-reported rules and verification labels.
Do not share private quiz answers, full chat transcript, social handles, exact address or contact details by default.
Supporters have read-only access to explicitly shared items; access can expire or be revoked.
Do not require a parent to approve an adult's housing decision.

8.2 Payments in V1
FMH does not collect or hold rent, deposits or brokerage in V1.
Users agree payment details directly with the relevant owner/agent and should verify the recipient and property before transferring money.
Never ask users to send deposits to FMH or to an unverified intermediary.
Encourage written terms and receipts; do not present general guidance as legal advice.
Warn against OTP sharing, pressure to pay before viewing/verification, suspiciously low prices and payment to unrelated accounts.
Any future payment integration requires legal/compliance review, a reputable payment partner, clear dispute processes and a unit-economics case.
8.3 Legal and market variability
Rental practices and legal obligations vary by location, tenancy type and agreement. The product should record what the poster states and disclose unknowns. Do not imply a standard deposit, standard brokerage or legally sufficient agreement. Have India-qualified counsel review terms, notices, moderation and payment-related flows.

11. Safety and moderation
Phone/email verification and rate limits.
Private-by-default profiles and explicit publish/unpublish controls.
Connection approval before contact details are revealed.
Report, block, mute, hide listing and account deletion controls.
Admin queue for suspicious listings, impersonation, harassment and payment scams.
Evidence collection that is proportionate and secure; defined retention policy.
Escalation playbook with response targets for high-risk reports.
No public reputation score for protected traits, no public shaming and no unsupported “safe person” badge.
Clear user-facing language: verification is a specific check, not a guarantee.
12. Privacy and legal readiness
India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025 should inform product design. The Rules were notified in November 2025 and include phased commencement; legal counsel must confirm which provisions and timelines apply at launch. See official MeitY material: https://www.meity.gov.in/documents/act-and-policies/digital-personal-data-protection-rules-2025-gDOxUjMtQWa and PIB summary: https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2190655&lang=2&reg=48.
Plain-language notice describing purposes, data categories, sharing, retention and user rights.
Separate consent for publishing profile, linking social account, sharing with supporter, and processing audio where relevant.
Collect minimum data needed; don't store raw voice notes or complete chat histories by default.
Access, correction, deletion, consent withdrawal and unpublish workflows.
Document AI and hosting subprocessors and what data each receives.
Encryption in transit, appropriate at-rest protection, least-privilege access, secrets management, backups and audit logs.
No use of private quiz responses to train general models without a separate informed opt-in.
Retention schedule for profiles, raw message references, audio/transcripts, reports, logs and consent records.
Counsel review of sensitive data, user age handling, verification, moderation, advertising, payments and rental-related claims.

15. Technical architecture
Recommended default: a modular monolith with a relational database and one API. The product does not need microservices, a vector database or complex event streaming at pilot stage.
Responsive web/PWA before native apps.
Managed authentication, relational database, object storage and hosting where affordable.
Channel adapters map web/messaging inputs to one canonical conversation API.
AI extraction produces schema-validated proposals; deterministic backend rules control writes and permissions.
Matching is a separate deterministic service/module with versioned weights and explanation records.
Scheduled job handles listing freshness, reminders and expired slots.
Email/in-app notifications first; add paid SMS/messaging only after value is measured.
Development, staging and production environments; migrations, backups, monitoring and rollback notes.
Company-controlled GitHub, domain, cloud, database and AI accounts. No critical infrastructure under a contractor's personal account.
Document data flows and third-party processors before enabling voice or messaging.

22. Final product thesis
The moat is not the chatbot or the quiz alone. It is the structured understanding of shared-living preferences, reliable listing operations, consent-based trust signals and the feedback loop connecting compatibility predictions to real housing outcomes.
The first version should prove three things: users can describe their needs naturally; FMH can convert those needs into useful and explainable matches; and hosts can keep listings current while arranging viewings with less effort. If those loops work in one Bengaluru cluster, the product has evidence to justify more languages, messaging integrations, monetisation and future cities.
